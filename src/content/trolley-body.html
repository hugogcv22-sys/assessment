AI Made Execution Cheap. That Just Made Your Taste Very Expensive.

Why the next wave of company building isn’t an engineering problem—it’s a judgment problem.

MM

MOJ MAHDARA

OCT 2026 • CONTINUATION OF EDITIONS #1–#4

Friday morning. My eyes are dry, my back is freezing up again, and I’m driving to my doctor to get steroid shots before a Lovable kickoff, a zillion back-to-back meetings, and an afternoon check-in with Gwyneth. Source: Raw Car Video (WhatsApp Feb 6)

In the middle of all that, I’ve been watching what’s happening inside our first cohort of 🔗 AI Maxxing (from Substack #4). Founders, operators, and executives are building things in forty-five minutes that used to take a product team three weeks. They are spinning up internal dashboards before their coffee cools, connecting Claude to messy data rooms, and shipping real workflows with their hands on the keyboard. Editorial Continuity: Picks up right after Substack #4

It is exhilarating. And it is exposing a completely new problem.

When building something took six months and two hundred thousand dollars, friction acted as a natural filter. You had to argue about the idea. You had to defend the budget. You had to be reasonably sure the thing was worth existing before anyone wrote a line of code. 🔗 Echoes "Building Fast vs. Building Right" (#1)

Today, the friction is gone.

Anyone on your team can turn a half-baked thought into a functioning prototype, a twenty-page strategy doc, or a fifty-piece content calendar by lunch.

We solved the execution bottleneck. And in doing so, we walked straight into a judgment bottleneck. Moj Cadence: Short High-Contrast Pivot

Velocity without direction is just expensive noise

I was reviewing an operating update from a founder recently. Their team was proud of how fast they were moving with AI. They had automated their outreach, launched three new landing pages, and generated weeks of social assets in forty-eight hours.

I asked one basic question: “What happened to conversion?”

Silence.

They had mistaken activity for progress. Because the tools made it effortless to produce, nobody stopped to ask whether the underlying point of view was actually sharp enough to care about.

There is a name for what I’m seeing across companies right now.

Signature Device: Substack #2 & #3 Lexicon Series

synthetic momentum | sin-ˈthe-tik mō-ˈmen-təm | noun

The illusion of operational progress created when a team uses AI to multiply output—dashboards, decks, workflows, and copy—without making a single hard strategic decision.

  • Characterized by: pristine Notion databases nobody reads, automated emails nobody replies to, and the specific exhaustion of a team reviewing ten times more work than they did in 2024 while revenue stays flat.
  • Often misdiagnosed as: "Founder Mode" or high productivity.
  • The actual fix: human curation, ruthless elimination, and a leader who knows what to throw in the trash.
  • See also: 🔗 cognitive debt (#2), 🔗 context shifting (#3), 🔗 automating dirty data (#4).

Failure is a data point. Don’t automate the dysfunction.

People who know me know that failure actually turns me on a little bit—because it means I’m learning something. Every time a project fails, it gives us a data point. It’s one less thing I have to wonder about. Is this the right way? No. Great. Now we try the next way. Verbatim Quote: S2E5 @ 02:01 & Riverside Jul 3 @ 00:06

The process of elimination is my favorite tool in business. Look at our own internal projects over the past year—from SEO to how we structure content. You blow through what doesn't work, depersonalize the dysfunction, and iterate until you find the signal. Verbatim Quote: Riverside Jul 3 @ 00:02 & 00:33

The danger with AI right now is that instead of using it to test and eliminate bad ideas faster, teams are using it to scale their existing confusion.

The founders who are winning—the ones we back at 🔗 Kinship (#1) and the operators I respect most—use AI to compress the grunt work so they can spend more time on the one thing an LLM cannot replicate: 🔗 taste (Pillar #2 in Edition #2).

And taste isn’t which font you pick in Figma. Taste is pattern recognition earned through scars. It’s reading the room when a deal is going sideways. It’s having a point of view specific enough that your audience feels a human being behind the screen before they finish the first sentence.

Edit harder than you prompt

If AI gives you eighty percent of the baseline in ten seconds, your job is no longer to be a factory. Your job is to be the Editor-in-Chief.

Remember: 🔗 What you don’t inspect, people don’t respect (#2 & Manifesto). And if you let an algorithm make your judgment calls, your audience will feel it before your metrics show it.

Use the machines for speed. Keep your hands on the steering wheel for meaning.

I’ll keep telling you what I find.

Moj
